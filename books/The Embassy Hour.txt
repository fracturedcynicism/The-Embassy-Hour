Chapter 1: Marigold
The call came at 2:47 in the morning.
Kabul was quiet. The wrong kind of quiet. The last American convoy had rolled south on Highway 1 three days ago and hadn't come back. The checkpoints were empty. The Afghan Army wasn't retreating or surrendering. It was just gone. The way salt disappears in water — no trace, no sound, just an absence where something solid used to be.
Detective Uttam Pranidhi reached for his phone in the dark.
He was staying in a guesthouse two streets off Flower Street — the kind of place that hadn't changed its sheets or its politics since the Soviet withdrawal and had outlasted two subsequent occupations by the careful practice of not being noticed. The window faced north. He had kept it open. Outside, the city that was always noise — generators, traffic, the distant percussion of a place that never fully trusted silence — had gone to something else. Not peace. The absence of effort. A city holding its breath, conserving itself for whatever came next.
A dog barked somewhere in the middle distance and then stopped. Nothing answered it.
The woman on the other end of the line was threading Pashto and Punjabi together, the way people do when they're trying very hard not to cry and the language of grief keeps switching on them. She would find a word in one, lose it, find it in the other. He waited. He had learned twenty years ago that interrupting grief produced the same result as interrupting evidence: you got a performance instead of a fact.
"My husband gave me this number," she said finally. Punjabi now, steadier. "He said if he didn't call by midnight, I should."
Past midnight. Nearly three hours past it.
Her name was Preetinder Kaur. Her husband, Tariq Siddiqui, a photojournalist, had walked into the Pakistani embassy at noon to surrender his passport for a wedding formality — a Sikh-Afghan woman marrying a Pakistani journalist, the paperwork alone a minor geopolitical negotiation, three embassy visits and four months of correspondence to produce a piece of paper that said what both of them already knew. He hadn't walked out.
"When did you last hear from him?"
"Eleven o'clock. He said the clerk was slow. He said not to worry." A pause. He could hear her deciding whether the next part was safe to say. "He said there was something he needed to do inside before he came out."
"What kind of something?"
"He didn't say. He never told me the work things. He said it was safer if I didn't know."
Pranidhi understood the logic. He also understood that a man who had maintained that precaution for years had done so because he had correctly estimated the risk. Which meant whatever Tariq had gone into that embassy to do, the danger had been precisely as high as he'd calculated.
"What word was he supposed to say?" Pranidhi asked. "When he was safe."
A pause. Then, quietly: "Marigold."
No word had come. Which was itself a word.
"I'll find him," Pranidhi said.
He almost believed it.
He was already pulling on his jacket. On the street the night air was warm and still, carrying dust and diesel and something harder to name — the smell of a city beginning to let go of things it can no longer carry. He stood on the pavement before getting into the waiting car and looked north. The mountains were invisible in the dark, but he had learned to feel their direction the way you learn a room: by what the air does near the walls.
Somewhere in that direction, eighteen kilometres closer than they had been a week ago, men were moving with patience and momentum through checkpoints that had stopped checking.
He got in the car and went to work.

Chapter 2: The Platter of War
Jalalabad fell before breakfast. No shots fired. The Taliban didn't need bullets anymore — America had done the work for them, packing up Bagram in the night like a tenant skipping rent, leaving the lights on.
Kabul was next. Everyone knew. Nobody said it.
Thirty kilometres. Then twenty. The number kept shrinking like the last days of a fever.
Pranidhi arrived at the Pakistani embassy on Wazir Akbar Khan Road to find the guards doubled and the staff halved. He had driven through five blocks of the city on the way and had seen: a family with one car and too much furniture trying to compress a second life into a Corolla, a burning tyre on a roundabout with nobody near it, and a policeman sitting on a kerb in full uniform with his boots off, looking at his feet with the concentration of a man who has decided the next thing he does will need to be decided without reference to everything that has come before. These were not the signs of a city under attack. They were the signs of a city making a calculation.
Beyond the blast walls the compound had gone into the register of a place that has been briefed on what is coming and has chosen heightened normality as its response — too much order, too little conversation, the institutional stillness of controlled panic. A Frontier Corps guard checked his credentials twice. The second time was not about thoroughness.
Inside, the reception hall smelled of floor cleaner and old paper and the faint electrical burn of equipment running hotter than it should. Three ceiling fans turned without cooling anything. Along the far wall, a row of plastic chairs held the week's uncompleted business: an old man with a document folder on his lap, two women sitting close together and carefully not talking, a boy of perhaps twelve who had fallen asleep against his mother's shoulder. All of them here for the ordinary machinery of movement — visas, passports, the documentation for crossing a border in the conventional direction. All of them in a room that was deciding, at an administrative level, whether those borders would still mean what they said by the time the paperwork was processed.
Dawit Karimi met him at the inner gate. Afghan police inspector, twenty-six, in a uniform that was clean in a way that required effort to maintain in this building at this moment. He had the eyes of someone who reads a room before entering it — a specific quality of attention that looked like stillness and was actually very fast.
"What do you know?" Dawit asked, falling into step.
"Not enough. Too much," Pranidhi said, and walked inside.
Dawit followed without asking what that meant. Which told Pranidhi something about him.
In the duty office, a file had been assembled: Tariq Siddiqui, forty-one, Pakistani national, press credentials from four international agencies. Entry logged at 12:04 PM by consular clerk Rehman. Assigned to processing corridor seven. No exit logged. No record of any secondary procedure that would account for the duration. A gap in the paperwork that should not exist in a building whose paperwork was its entire architecture.
Attached to the file: a photograph. Tariq at some earlier event, standing among journalists, taller than most of them, wearing a camera vest with too many pockets. He was laughing at something outside the frame. The laugh had reached his eyes. Pranidhi noted this and closed the file.
The UN Security Council had already been notified. An unsolved disappearance inside a diplomatic mission — Article 22, Vienna Convention — in a country mid-collapse, with Pakistan, India, and China all watching. Careers would end over this. Ambassadors would be expelled. Alliances would fracture along fault lines that had been forming for a decade. And the men who wanted chaos most were eighteen kilometres away and closing.
Interpol had given Pranidhi twelve hours.
He had six suspects, no body, and a building that was slowly being abandoned around him — two junior staff had already called in sick, which in Kabul that week was a way of saying goodbye.
"Walk me through the building," he said to Dawit.
Dawit walked him through the building. Administrative wing: left off the lobby, six offices, all functional, all on camera. Ambassador's suite with a connecting door that reached the east corridor without passing through the lobby — Pranidhi noted this. Service entrance at the building's far end, accessible from the east corridor and from the external street without passing through the main gate — he noted this too. Trade attaché office: first floor, recently moved from the third. And right off the lobby, the consular section: processing corridor with seven rooms, one exit through a service passage, and the camera covering the corridor's entrance that had been non-functional for six weeks.
He started with the cameras.

Chapter 3: INGSOC
The embassy's surveillance system covered eleven of its fourteen rooms. The consular section — where Tariq had last been seen alive — was one of three exceptions. Maintenance fault, said the duty officer. Logged six weeks ago. Never repaired.
Pranidhi noted the date the fault was logged. Circled it. Six weeks was not a coincidence. Someone had planned for this room to be blind before they knew they would need it blind. That kind of planning has a name: premeditation. That kind of premeditation requires access. Access requires trust. Trust, in an embassy, requires rank.
He filed that thought and went back to the cameras that did work.
The embassy's layout had already told him something useful. The lobby opened into two wings: left, the administrative offices, covered by six functional cameras. Right, the consular corridor — one entrance from the lobby, one exit through a service passage at the building's far end. The camera at the consular entrance was the one that had failed. Whatever had happened to Tariq had happened inside a box with no eyes on either door.
He sat in the review room for four hours. It was a repurposed storage space on the ground floor — two chairs, a monitor, a socket strip running cables to the hard drives that fed the working cameras. The room smelled of old circuit boards and the particular dust of electronics kept in spaces nobody aired. Pranidhi sat in the better chair and watched.
The lobby footage told him this: Tariq Siddiqui entered at 12:04 PM — camera bag over one shoulder, moving with the unhurried confidence of a man who had covered three wars and found passport bureaucracy beneath his dignity. He paused at the reception desk for forty seconds — long enough to give his name and be recognised, not long enough to fill out anything. Then consular clerk Rehman appeared at the inner door and held it open.
Pranidhi watched Rehman's hands while he held the door. Steady. Practised steadiness, but the kind that requires maintenance.
At 12:31, Rehman returned to the lobby alone. He paused at the water cooler for eleven seconds — long enough to look like he was drinking, too short to actually swallow — then moved to the filing room. Pranidhi ran the file room timestamp: Rehman entered at 12:31:44 and exited at 12:35:51. Four minutes and seven seconds in a room with rows of dead-document binders and a false-panel cavity in the south wall that he would know about because his desk was twelve feet from it and he had been working in this building for three years.
Pranidhi rewound to the water cooler. Watched Rehman's eleven seconds again. The cooler was positioned at the lobby's northwest corner, adjacent to the waiting area seating. At 12:33 — two minutes after Rehman returned from the filing room and was back at his desk — a woman entered the lobby.
She cited a lost document reference to the duty officer, delivering the information with the specific fluency of someone who has practised saying it until it no longer sounds practised. She took a seat in the waiting area, facing forward. Over ninety minutes she did not look at her phone. She did not read the magazine she picked up and then put back. She looked at the middle distance with the careful incuriosity of someone watching a specific thing and very much not wanting to appear to be watching it.
At 12:31 — while Rehman was still in the filing room, before this woman had entered — the brush pass had not yet happened.
He underlined in his notebook: She came in two minutes after Rehman returned. The pass was on his way back to his desk, not on the way to the filing room. She passed him something at the cooler after he had already been to the room. So what did he do in the filing room without the key she was bringing him?
He sat with the question long enough for it to acquire a different shape.
The filing room was where the records were kept. The records room — adjacent, same corridor — was where the false panel was. Rehman hadn't gone to collect anything. He had gone to confirm something was still there.
In the corridor outside the review room, Dawit was leaning against the wall with his arms crossed, watching the light change through the building's only north-facing window. The sound coming through the walls was different from an hour ago. Not louder. More directional.
"We have maybe one day," Dawit said when Pranidhi came out.
"Then I'll need one day."

Chapter 4: Chaturanga
Tariq had not come to surrender a passport. That was cover.
Three weeks earlier, embedded with a supply convoy pushing north through Kunduz, he had photographed a meeting in a compound off the main road: ISI handlers and Taliban field commanders around a folding table, coordinates exchanged on paper rather than phone, the careful analogue tradecraft of men who knew they were being listened to digitally. Hand-drawn maps. A satellite photograph with three points circled in blue ink. The kind of evidence that ends careers, breaks alliances, and gets people killed in embassy processing rooms.
He had arranged to pass the photographs to Pranidhi through a dead drop — the camera bag, left in a specific location inside the building. The passport formality was his reason to enter. The bag was his reason to be there at all.
Someone had found out. Someone had been ready.
Pranidhi worked the six suspects over two days with the patience of a man who has learned that pressure produces performance, not truth, and with a technique he had used eight times in twenty years without it failing him yet.
A canary trap.
He fed each suspect a slightly different version of what he knew.
The first conversation was with Rehman. He had asked Dawit to stay outside for this one — not because he needed privacy, but because Rehman's fear had a specific quality that other people's presence amplified in unproductive directions. He needed the man nervous, not performing nervousness.
Rehman sat across the table with his hands in his lap, which was the position of someone who had decided that if his hands were not visible they could not give him away, and who did not yet understand that the decision to hide them was already the information.
"I want to understand the processing corridor," Pranidhi said. "Walk me through a standard intake."
Rehman walked him through it. Standard intake, visitor checked in at desk, escort to room seven for document submission, visitor waits for verification, clerk returns, issue processed. Standard. Fifteen minutes, twenty at most for complex cases.
"And if the visitor brings supplementary material? Documents, items for collection?"
"They'd be logged. Receipted."
"Tariq Siddiqui's log."
Rehman looked at the table. "No supplementary material logged."
"A witness saw two people enter the processing corridor," Pranidhi said. "Not one."
A pause that lasted slightly longer than it should have. "I don't know anything about that."
"I didn't ask you a question," Pranidhi said. He moved on to the filing system and watched Rehman's left hand move three centimetres toward his right while he answered. The body going toward something to hold. The mind stopping it.
Afterwards, walking to the next interview, he wrote in the margin of his notes: The hand move. South wall of the filing room. He knows what's behind the panel. He's been looking at it for two days.
The second conversation was with Colonel Farhan Afridi, the trade attaché. Military posture, practised smile, the particular watchfulness of a man who has spent his career in rooms where the official purpose is never the actual purpose. He arrived early. He sat without invitation. He crossed one knee over the other and arranged his face into the expression of a man who has nothing to hide because he has already planned what to show.
"A terrible business," Afridi said. "I hope we can resolve this quickly. The building has other concerns right now."
"The forensics team has dated the camera fault," Pranidhi said, without looking up from his notes. "Four weeks ago, not six."
The silence lasted one and a half seconds.
Afridi's hands were on the table. They didn't move. His face didn't move. But something behind his eyes recalculated — the way a man runs the numbers when the numbers he's been given don't match the numbers he knows are right — and in that gap, in that one and a half seconds, Pranidhi had everything he needed.
"That's consistent with what the duty officer reported," Afridi said. His voice hadn't changed pitch. "I couldn't speak to the technical specifics."
"Of course not," Pranidhi said. "Thank you for your time."
Afridi left the way he had entered. Unhurried. Clean.
Pranidhi waited until the door closed before writing a single word in his notebook.
Bird.
The third conversation was with Yusuf Haidari, who came in with a folded newspaper and his sleeves already rolled, and sat down with the manner of a man who has had a day-long debate with himself about how much to volunteer and has arrived having rehearsed both sides. He talked for twelve minutes. He talked about building procedures and staff schedules and camera maintenance cycles, and while he talked Pranidhi watched his eyes move twice to the window and twice to the door and not once to his face. He was building something — not a lie, but a frame inside which a truth could sit without being seen directly.
Pranidhi interrupted him to say, casually, that Tariq's camera bag had been found in the building.
Haidari absorbed this. "That's — good news, presumably?"
"Presumably," Pranidhi said, and changed the subject.
Haidari's eyes went to his face for the first time. Just once. And then away.
The fourth conversation was with Nadia Osman, the Afghan nurse who had been waiting for three months on a visa renewal in a country that was quietly ceasing to issue them. She came in with a glass of water she had brought herself and sat with her hands around it on the table, and she answered every question directly and without qualification and in such complete sentences that Pranidhi initially read it as scripted and then understood it was simply how she spoke — the habit of someone whose professional life required precision about things that had consequences.
He told her, with appropriate gravity, that the ambassador himself had been questioned.
"As a suspect?" she said.
"As part of the process."
She thought about this for a moment. Not with surprise — with the specific thoughtfulness of someone revising a prior model. "Then it goes higher than a clerk," she said. Not to Pranidhi. To herself.
"You should be careful about conclusions," Pranidhi said.
"I know." She finished her water. "I've been here three months. I've had time to think."
The fifth conversation was with Samira Ghilzai. He offered her the same interview room, the same chair, the same glass of water. He told her he had a few routine questions. Then he said nothing. He waited.
She waited too. With the composure of someone who had been trained to wait — not patience, exactly, but the professional equivalent of it: a settled readiness that had nothing to prove. She treated the silence the way she had treated the ninety minutes in the waiting room, the way she had treated the false document reference — as furniture. Present in the room, requiring nothing from her.
He thanked her for her time.
Different seeds. Different birds. Whoever sang back the wrong note had been talking to someone they shouldn't.
Dawit sat outside each interview room and watched the faces coming out. He didn't need to hear the questions. He had spent six years reading exits — the way a person holds a door when they want to leave a room faster than their body is already moving, the way the eyes go somewhere private for a half-second before resetting to neutral. After the fifth interview he wrote two words in his notebook and circled them. He didn't show Pranidhi until later. He wasn't ready to show Pranidhi until he was sure.
The Taliban took the eastern approach road that evening. Pranidhi heard it on the duty officer's radio — a burst of Pashto, a pause, then the officer switching frequencies with his face doing nothing whatsoever. Nobody in the building mentioned it. Nobody had to. You could hear it in the way people moved through the corridors for the next hour — slightly faster, slightly quieter, the small adjustments of bodies that have received information their minds haven't yet processed but their feet have already responded to.
The number was down to eighteen kilometres. Then sixteen. The fever running its last calculation.

Chapter 5: Trojan Horse
By the second evening the embassy's internal phone log showed a call placed from Haidari's extension at 11:47 PM to an outside number that routed through three exchanges before resolving. Pranidhi had seen that routing pattern before. RAW — Indian foreign intelligence. The camera bag detail had come back through channels it should never have reached.
Haidari wasn't an overhelpful clerk.
He was Pranidhi's own contact inside the building, placed six weeks before the investigation began, before anyone knew an investigation would be needed. The man had been running protective surveillance on Pranidhi's behalf since the day Tariq walked in. Which meant the canary seed planted with Haidari — the camera bag has been found — had not been the leak. He had called Delhi through a clean channel with something else. Something Pranidhi hadn't given him.
He sat with that for a long moment. Haidari had been protecting a channel, not a secret. The call to RAW was not a betrayal — it was a handoff. Haidari had something that needed to reach somewhere that could act on it before Kabul made the action irrelevant, and he had used the architecture Pranidhi had given him to send it.
The question was what he'd sent.
He called for the second interview.
Haidari arrived with tea. He set one cup in front of Pranidhi, one in front of himself, and sat with the economy of a man who has rehearsed the moment until the props have become natural. He folded his hands on the table. He looked at Pranidhi with the particular attention of someone who has been deciding for twenty-four hours and has now decided.
"You know what I'm going to ask," Pranidhi said.
"Yes." Haidari looked at the tea. "I've been deciding whether to say it since yesterday morning."
"What decided you?"
"The corridor." He didn't elaborate. He meant the east corridor at 23:58 — a thing he had seen from the stairwell that he was not supposed to have seen because he was not supposed to have been in the building at that hour.
"The camera in the consular section," Haidari said quietly. "It was working when Tariq came in. I watched Rehman disable it afterward. But Rehman doesn't have the access code."
"Who does?"
Haidari looked at the connecting door to the adjacent office. Then back. His voice dropped another register.
"The Ambassador."
Outside, a generator kicked on somewhere in the building. The lights had been flickering for an hour. The city's power grid was failing in sections, suburb by suburb, as if Kabul were a man bleeding out slowly. The fluorescent strip above them pulsed once, held, pulsed again.
"You should have told me earlier," Pranidhi said.
"Yes," Haidari said. He did not apologise for it.
Pranidhi dismissed him and sat alone for a moment in the flickering room. The question wasn't why Haidari had waited. The question was what he had been protecting while he waited — and whether protecting it had been the right call, and whether Pranidhi was in any position to judge a man for caution, sitting in this building in this city at this hour.
He picked up his notebook and went to find the Ambassador.

Chapter 6: Nightingale
Dawit found Pranidhi on the floor of the east corridor at 23:58, alive by a margin that wouldn't survive close measurement.
No preamble. No approach. Just the floor, and Pranidhi on it, and the specific silence of a corridor that has recently held violence and is now pretending otherwise.
Nadia Osman was already crouched beside him. The woman from the waiting room — expired visa, four chairs from the processing corridor, the magazine she had never opened. She had been in this building for three months and four days on a visa renewal for a country that had quietly stopped issuing renewals three weeks ago, and she had stayed because the building was known to her and Kabul outside was becoming unknown in a way she didn't yet have a map for. She was thirty-one. Six years a field trauma nurse, MSF hospital outside Kunduz, until the hospital was shelled and her reassignment order was processed into a paper trail that ended in a consular waiting room.
She had heard the sound from the corridor and walked toward it rather than away from it. That was the distinction that separated her, Dawit would later think, from approximately thirty-nine of every forty people.
Her scarf was pressed against Pranidhi's side. She was talking to him low and steady in Dari, the register nurses use when what they are actually doing is keeping a person tethered to consciousness by the sound of another voice — not because the words matter, but because stopping would matter.
Her hands were dark with it. She didn't seem to notice.
Three metres along the wall, Ambassador Qureshi sat breathing in the shallow way of a man whose body has issued a verdict his mind is still appealing. He had been looking at a fixed point on the floor between his feet since before Dawit arrived, and he was going to go on looking at it. He made one attempt to speak when Dawit entered the corridor — a small preparatory sound, the beginning of something — and then closed his mouth again with the expression of a man who has understood that no version of the next sentence helps him.
Dawit looked at Pranidhi. At the floor. At the distance between where he lay and the connecting door to the Ambassador's office, which was ajar, which had not been ajar an hour ago when Dawit had last passed this corridor. The connecting door was an architectural fact he had noted on the first walkthrough and filed under: interesting, irrelevant. It was relevant now.
He did not ask what happened. The corridor had already answered that.
Pranidhi opened his eyes. Found Dawit's face with the focus of a man spending something he has a limited supply of.
"Notebook," he said. "Jacket."
Dawit reached in. Found it. Found the torn page tucked separately — the number with the pencilled star, underlined twice.
"Ambassador," Pranidhi said.
"I know."
Pranidhi put his head back against the wall with the expression of a man who has handed off a weight he can no longer carry and has made his peace with that.
Dawit turned to Nadia. She was still talking under her breath, the low Dari continuing even as her eyes came to his face. She had the quality of someone who could do two things at once not because they were easy but because she had practised until they weren't separate.
"I need to ask you about Rehman."
She thought for a moment. Precise, not hesitant. "The woman with the document claim. At the water cooler. She bumped into him. I thought she'd dropped something and he picked it up. Small. Metal. Gone in under a second."
"A room key."
Nadia nodded slowly. Not surprised. Nurses learn early that the world is mostly explicable once you have enough of the facts. The facts were almost always there. You just had to know which ones to collect.
She looked at Dawit steadily. Through the window behind him, the sound of vehicles moving on the street below. Not stopping. Not slowing.
"Will it matter?" she said. "What I saw. With everything that's—" She didn't finish. She looked at the window instead, at the specific quality of night that had settled over the city in the last hour, which was different from the night that had been there before.
Dawit thought of the torn page in his hand. Of the annotated star. Of the forty minutes he had before the Security Council session Pranidhi would not be attending.
"It already has," he said.

Chapter 7: The Sands of Time
He read the notebook in the ambassador's office with Qureshi still breathing on the far side of the room and the sound of Kabul rearranging itself filtering through the walls. Pranidhi's handwriting was compressed — the script of a man rationing time.
Rehman, twenty-nine. Family in Lahore, mother unwell, monthly remittances sent on a salary that doesn't quite cover them. During interview, eyes moved twice to the connecting door. Not afraid of me. Afraid of something behind it. Leveraged. Someone found his price and paid it.
Dawit looked up from the notebook. Across the room, Qureshi had not changed position. He was a man of perhaps sixty with the bearing of someone who had spent a career performing authority and had reached a moment where the performance had failed and the thing underneath it — which was smaller than the performance suggested — was now visible. He breathed. More than that was unclear.
Afridi. Left at 12:55 — logged, timestamped, gate guard corroborated. Too clean. His vehicle re-entered through the service entrance at 13:40. Not in the gate log. Service entrance has no camera. Connects directly to the processing corridor. He was inside when it happened.
The brush pass at the water cooler — Samira to Rehman, 12:31, lobby camera. Not documents. Too small. A room key. Processing room locked from inside. Twenty minutes. Then Afridi back through the service entrance, out through the front, timestamped, alibi intact.
Dawit thought about the architecture. The service entrance he had walked past twice without needing to think about it. The processing corridor with no camera on either end. The false panel in the records room, the camera bag behind it. Every element of this had been placed — not improvised. The camera fault logged six weeks before. The service entrance memorised, the gate log known to be unsupervised. Someone had mapped this building with the specific attention of someone planning to use it.
Camera bag in the records room — behind the false panel. Rehman's eyes went there twice in the first interview. He couldn't stop looking at what he was protecting.
Qureshi gave Rehman the access code. Did not originate the order. Too frightened to give orders — takes them. Follow the chain up.
He paused on that entry. Qureshi had given the access code. That was the thing Haidari had been carrying — not the code itself, but the knowledge of who had given it, which was a different weight entirely. It meant the chain ran through the ambassador and through the trade attaché and through the ISI logistics operative in the waiting room, and somewhere at the top of that chain was a decision that had been made before Tariq Siddiqui had known he was going to walk into this building.
Samira — ISI logistics designation, flagged Karachi 2019, never apprehended. Did not come to act. Came to confirm. Which means the actor was already inside.
Calling the meeting for tomorrow. All principals. Reception hall. If I'm not there by 2:30 — Karimi finds me first. He's clean. I think. But I didn't survive this long by thinking.
Dawit closed the notebook.
He sat for a moment with it in his hands. Pranidhi had written he's clean as a provisional assessment, a man who maintained the professional discipline of noting what he believed while acknowledging the epistemological limits of believing. He had been doing this long enough to know that certainty was a luxury only available to people who didn't need to be right about things. He had been in this building for two days and had decided that Dawit was clean based on his exits — on what he did when he came out of rooms, on what his face did when it thought nobody was measuring it.
Dawit thought this was probably correct. He also thought that a man who trusted his own judgment about another man's trustworthiness was the specific kind of man who got killed in east corridors.
Through the window, two blocks south, figures were moving in the street with rifles across their backs. Not running. Walking, with the unhurried authority of men who have already been told this is theirs and are now simply moving through it, taking possession of it in the way of people who have waited a long time and have decided they can afford to be unhurried about the last hundred metres.
He had the notebook. He had forty minutes before the Security Council session. He had a dead detective, a dying ambassador, and five suspects in the next room who believed they were waiting for a meeting that was never going to start. He had a camera bag whose contents he hadn't yet examined but which Pranidhi had marked as the specific thing worth dying for a room's distance from.
He set the notebook on the desk.
He worked fast.

Chapter 8: Voyeurism
The camera bag was exactly where Pranidhi had written — false panel, records room, the room Rehman's eyes had moved to twice in interview. The panel was a file-storage cavity in the wall, the kind installed in older embassy buildings to accommodate document safes that had been decommissioned. The bag had been pushed to the cavity's back and covered with a stack of binders. Someone had found the hiding place without finding the bag. Or had chosen not to remove it.
He opened it on the records room floor.
The camera was a professional field body, worn at the grip from years of use. The lens had an impact crack at the filter ring wrapped in electrical tape and then ignored — the working practice of a man who repaired what failed and kept going. The memory cards were in an interior pocket, labelled in a shorthand Dawit couldn't fully parse. He didn't need to. He had a laptop from the duty office and the cards read cleanly.
Kunduz. A compound off the main road — the angle told him Tariq had been shooting from elevation, distance, long lens, probably from a vehicle roof at the edge of the road. The subjects were visible through a gap in the compound wall, gathered around a folding table under a corrugated roof.
Four men on one side. Three on the other. The four: civilian clothes, but the posture and the watchfulness of men for whom civilian clothes are a professional accommodation. The three: Taliban field uniform, two of them with the specific look of men who have recently come from somewhere active.
On the table: paper maps, hand-drawn. A satellite photograph, printed, with three points circled in blue ink. Coordinates being exchanged by hand, on paper, without phones, without signals. The careful analogue tradecraft of men who understand they are being listened to digitally and have planned accordingly.
And in the background of a second frame — Tariq re-angled slightly between shots — a whiteboard on the compound's interior wall. Numbers in red marker. Grid coordinates in a format Dawit had been trained to read.
He read them twice.
Bagram Airfield.
Not a target. A transfer point.
The Americans had left in the night three days ago without briefing their Afghan partners — pulled out of the largest military installation in the country, sixty kilometres north of Kabul, and left behind an inventory that had not been inventoried on the way out. Tens of thousands of rifles. Black Hawk helicopters still on the flight line. Night-vision equipment. Armoured vehicles. Artillery. Enough materiel to equip an army, or to sell one. Left in hardened bunkers with functional power and no one watching the gate.
Kabul had been the distraction. The embassy, the disappearance, the international incident — all of it drawing the camera west, toward the story everyone already knew, while the real prize sat sixty kilometres north, patient and enormous, waiting to belong to whoever got there first with enough trucks and enough time.
The window to secure it was closing by the hour.
Tariq had understood this. He had not gone into the Pakistani embassy to expose an ISI-Taliban meeting. He had gone to pass the coordinates — the background detail in the second frame that the subjects hadn't bothered to erase from their whiteboard, which told him they hadn't understood what they'd allowed to be seen.
Someone had understood what he'd seen. And had been ready.
Dawit set the photographs in order, put them back in the bag, and went to find Rehman.

Chapter 9: House of Cards
He found him at his desk in the empty admin office on the second floor. Not working. Just sitting with his hands flat on the desk and his eyes somewhere between the desk and the window, in the posture of a man whose body has decided that the minimum viable configuration is upright in a chair and is maintaining it at increasing cost.
The rest of the staff had gone home or somewhere. The room smelled of burned coffee and the particular staleness of a building being vacated in stages — thinned out incrementally, the air changing too slowly for any individual departure to register, until suddenly the remaining people notice how much has already left.
Rehman looked up when Dawit entered. His face had the texture of a man who had not slept in three days because sleep requires some minimum of hope.
Dawit placed Samira Ghilzai's photograph on the desk and sat down across from him without pulling the chair out first.
Rehman looked at the photograph. Something in him — the structure that had been holding him vertical — simply gave way. Not drama. Not tears. Just the quiet, complete collapse of something load-bearing that has distributed the weight in the wrong direction for the last time.
"He came to me in March," Rehman said. "Not Afridi. Someone before Afridi. He had information about my brother. About something my brother did in 2019 in Lahore that I can't — that I won't talk about. He said all I needed to do was provide access when asked. He said probably nothing would ever be asked."
"Something was asked."
"In June. Afridi came to me in June. He said there would be an audit — security, internal, unofficial. He said I should disable the consular camera and give him the room code. He said no one would know." Rehman looked at his hands on the desk. "I knew it wasn't an audit. I gave him the code anyway."
"The morning Tariq came in."
"Afridi told me there would be a journalist. He said the man had taken something that didn't belong to him." Rehman's voice had the flatness of a rehearsed testimony meeting its actual performance — all the feeling burned out in the preparation, only the words left. "He said I needed to escort him to corridor seven and come back. He said whatever happened after was not my concern."
"He said no one would be hurt."
Rehman looked at him. "How did you know that?"
"Because they always say it will be clean."
A silence. Rehman's hands moved on the desk — not the concealing movement from the first interview, but something else, smaller, like a man doing inventory.
"He said the journalist had stolen something. He used the word recovered. He said he needed help recovering something that belonged to Pakistan."
"The camera bag."
"I didn't know what was in it. I still don't." He paused. "I know it doesn't matter that I didn't know."
"No," Dawit said. "It doesn't."
Outside, somewhere, a burst of automatic fire, then the specific silence that follows when everyone is deciding what to do next rather than responding to what just happened. Rehman didn't flinch. He had been hearing it intermittently for hours and had distributed it into the background, the way the body processes sounds it cannot act on.
"Where is Afridi now?" Dawit said.
Rehman looked at the ceiling for a moment. Finding the last thing. "He didn't leave."
Dawit was already moving.

Chapter 10: Close Encounters
The service entrance was at the far end of the east corridor — the same corridor where Pranidhi lay, where Qureshi was bleeding out against the wall, where the building's one remaining security guard had last been seen twenty minutes ago and hadn't been seen since.
The lights were on emergency power. Every thirty seconds they flickered — a half-second of darkness in which anything could happen, and the corridor was long enough that the far end was always in partial shadow regardless. Dawit had walked this corridor three times since arriving in the building. He had not previously needed to think about what it would look like as a tactical problem.
He thought about it now.
He moved with his back to the wall, pistol drawn, left shoulder brushing the plaster. The training came back in the order it was needed — not as a sequence but as a set of instincts activating in response to specific stimuli. Corners first. Doorways second. Open space last. Moving targets in long corridors cost the shooter something. Static ones cost nothing.
The corridor smelled of cleaning fluid and the faint copper undertone that had been in the air since he found Pranidhi. From outside, through the building's thick walls, the city produced a low continuous sound that had changed character in the past two hours — less like traffic, more like weather, the ambient noise of something large moving through an area not built for it.
First door on his left: supplies closet, open, empty. He held at the frame, cleared left and right, moved on.
Second door: bathroom. He pushed it wide with his foot and stepped back. Empty. The light inside was on and not flickering — a different circuit. He noted this.
The corridor's far end remained in partial shadow. He watched it for three full seconds without moving. Nothing moved back.
He covered the next twenty metres in measured intervals, stopping at each dark patch between the emergency light strips. The discipline of not rushing toward the unknown was something he had learned as a child, in a different context, and had found applicable to an unreasonable number of subsequent situations.
Thirty seconds. The lights flickered. A full second of black.
He held position. Count — one, two — and kept the barrel at shoulder height in the direction of the far end, knowing that in the darkness he was as invisible as whatever was there, and that the advantage went to whoever was calmer about not firing. He was calm. He had been in worse dark than this with less certainty about what was in it.
The lights returned.
Afridi was at the far end of the corridor.
Not running. Standing. His jacket was off, folded over his left forearm with the care of a man who had removed it deliberately. His sidearm was visible on his hip. His hands were open at his sides in the posture of a man performing calm — not feeling it, performing it, which was a different thing and required a different kind of response.
"Inspector Karimi." His voice carried the corridor's acoustics cleanly, as if he had done this in this space or one very much like it. "You're a long way outside your jurisdiction."
"Where's the guard?"
"Resting. He's fine." A pause in which Afridi made a decision about how much to continue. "You know what you've found in that bag. You know what it means. The question is what you do with it."
"The question," Dawit said, "is whether you come out from that end of the corridor or I come to you."
Afridi smiled. Not warmly. With the professional appreciation of a man who has just been told something that confirms his opponent is both serious and informed. He took one step forward. Stopped.
"You understand what's in those photographs," Afridi said. "You're a policeman in a city that won't have Afghan police by this time tomorrow. Whatever you think you're protecting — the process, the investigation, the international referral — all of that ends at midnight. Maybe sooner."
"The photographs don't end," Dawit said.
"Evidence is only evidence until the jurisdiction that receives it decides what to do with it. And those decisions happen in rooms you'll never be in."
"Keep talking," Dawit said. "Or start walking."
Afridi's right hand moved half a centimetre toward his hip.
The lights flickered.
Dawit dropped to one knee, barrel up. The darkness lasted one second — long enough for a shot, long enough to die. He heard nothing in it. Not a footstep, not the scrape of leather on a holster, not the compressed sound of a man who has made a decision and is acting on it. Trained men don't telegraph. The silence told him precisely nothing.
He held position. Barrel up. Floor cold through his knee. The diesel rumble from outside had grown in the last thirty seconds — he could feel it through the wall now, multiple vehicles, not stopping, the low continuous movement of a convoy that had somewhere to be.
When the light returned Afridi's hand was still moving and Dawit's voice cut across the corridor sharp and flat:
"Don't."
One word. The weight of it was in what came after it: nothing. No second warning. No negotiation. The word had the quality of a position stated without emotion, which is the most dangerous version of a position.
Afridi's hand stopped.
For five seconds neither of them moved. Through the corridor's single high window, the convoy's headlights swept the wall in a moving bar of white — multiple vehicles, fast, not stopping for anything. Close enough now that the engine sounds had separated into individual sources.
Afridi looked at the window. Then at Dawit. The calculation in his face was visible as a physical process — not fear, not defeat, but arithmetic. A man updating his model with new variables and arriving at a different answer than the one he had entered the corridor with.
He put his hands up.
Dawit crossed the corridor in eight steps, took the sidearm, had Afridi against the wall with his wrists secured before the man could run a second calculation. Up close, Afridi smelled of sweat and something medicinal — adrenaline metabolising, the body processing what the mind was still negotiating.
Afridi said nothing while Dawit worked. His face had gone to the blank professional expression of a man who has entered the portion of the situation where speech no longer improves his position. He wore it the way he had worn the performed calm — deliberately, as a tool, even when the tool was no longer doing anything.
"Samira said you can't stop what's already moving," Dawit said.
"She was right."
"She's under arrest. So are you." Dawit pulled him toward the corridor. "Keep moving."

Chapter 11: Relinquish
Dawit transmitted everything at six in the morning — Pranidhi's notebook, the photographs, the service log, Rehman's statement, Nadia's witness account, Afridi's arrest report — through the number in Pranidhi's belongings annotated with a pencilled star, which resolved to a RAW duty station in Delhi that picked up on the first ring. Not the second. The first, as if the line had been open and waiting for exactly this call.
They had been waiting. He understood now what Haidari had been protecting — not a person, not a secret, but the channel itself. The specific number and routing that would ensure what Pranidhi had assembled arrived somewhere capable of acting on it before Kabul made all of it historical rather than operational.
The building was quieter than it had been at any point since he had arrived. The staff who had called in sick had been right to. The two guards who remained had positioned themselves near the main entrance and were doing the specific kind of guard work that is really just witnessing — being present while something happens so that it can later be said that someone was present when it did.
Samira Ghilzai was arrested in the reception hall without incident, sitting in the same chair she had occupied for the last four hours, as if she had decided the chair was hers regardless of outcome and intended to remain in it. She looked at Dawit without expression when he came to stand in front of her — not the performed calm that Afridi had worn, but actual stillness, the kind that comes from having understood from the beginning that this portion of the operation was designed to be expendable.
"You can't stop what's already moving," she said again.
"You keep saying that," Dawit said. "Start telling me what's moving."
She looked past him at the window, at the early light coming in over the blast walls with the specific flatness of a morning that knows it is the first morning of something new and has not yet decided whether that is its business to announce.
She said nothing. He hadn't expected her to. The conversation she would eventually have — in a facility in Delhi or Lyon or somewhere unnamed and fluorescent — would not begin with his question. It would begin with the whiteboard coordinates. With the red marker numbers that Tariq Siddiqui had framed in the background of his second shot, at the cost of everything.
He left her in her chair and went to find Nadia.

Chapter 12: Swan Song
Dawit stood at the second-floor window as the light came fully up and watched the city complete its transformation.
Government vehicles moving south in convoy — not fleeing, exactly, but the specific efficient movement of institutions that have made their calculation and are executing it with the residual professionalism of people who were, until recently, professionals. New flags appearing on buildings two blocks east. White, unmistakable, going up fast and without ceremony on a building he knew had been a ministry annex the day before yesterday. Then a block closer. Then across the intersection. Then the building diagonally opposite, which he had been watching for ten minutes — a man on the roof, moving with purpose, the old things coming down and the new things going up in the same motion, as if they had always been meant to replace each other and the only question had been timing.
He had grown up twelve kilometres from where he was standing. He had walked the streets below on the way to school, on the way to the police college, on the way to dozens of ordinary things that were ordinary in a way that required a certain kind of city to exist. He was watching that city being replaced by a different city in the same location, and the replacement was happening with a logistics that suggested it had been planned for some time.
Nadia Osman was in the corridor behind him, sitting on the floor with her back to the wall, still in the bloodstained clothes, waiting. He had offered her water. She had taken it and put it down beside her without drinking. She was waiting for the next thing to become clear — not passively, the way frightened people wait, but with the specific quality of someone who has processed the situation and is simply letting time pass until the next decision presents itself. She had nowhere to be that was better than here. She had learned three months ago to stop attaching the concept of next week to anything specific.
His phone rang. Interpol, Lyon.
"We have the ISI connection," the duty officer said. "Enough for the Security Council." A pause — the register of someone choosing words carefully on a line that is recorded on both ends. "There's something else. Bagram. We flagged Siddiqui's coordinates to CENTCOM. They've confirmed the inventory discrepancy at the site." A beat. "What's still on that base—"
"How much?" Dawit said.
The number was not a small number. It was the kind of number that produced silence on the receiving end — not confusion, but the specific silence of comprehension arriving faster than any useful response.
He looked north. Toward the mountains visible from the window. Toward the airfield sixty kilometres beyond them that he couldn't see but could feel — the way you feel a door left open in a house you're supposed to be guarding. The weapons inventory sitting in hardened bunkers in the early flat light of an Afghan morning, patient, enormous, indifferent to the question of who would arrive first.
"Can it be secured?"
The pause that followed lasted long enough to be its own answer.
"We're working on it," the officer said.
Dawit lowered the phone.
He thought about the number he had for a woman named Preetinder Kaur. About what it would mean to call it. About what word he could give her that would do the work Marigold was supposed to do — carry the same information, the same relief, from a different person, under different circumstances. He didn't have a word. He filed that alongside everything else he didn't have answers for, which was a long list and was going to stay long, and which he would have to carry out of this building regardless of its length.
Outside, the flags were closer than they had been ten minutes ago. Street by street, block by block, with the steady momentum of something that has waited a long time and is no longer in a hurry because waiting is over and what comes next is administrative. On a runway sixty kilometres north, in the early flat light of an Afghan morning, an inventory of American weapons sat in hardened bunkers — patient, indifferent, ready to belong to whoever arrived first.
His phone showed two missed calls from numbers he didn't recognise.
He didn't answer.
Behind him, Nadia said: "What happens now?"
He thought about Tariq Siddiqui's photograph in the camera bag. The laugh that had reached his eyes. The camera vest with too many pockets. The long lens and the elevated position and the two minutes he would have understood he had and the decision he would have made about how to spend them.
He didn't have an answer for Nadia.
He looked north and waited for the sound of helicopters, not yet sure which kind would arrive first.
